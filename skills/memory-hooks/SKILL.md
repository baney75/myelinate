# Memory Hooks

A memory hook is a deliberately built retrieval cue for material that has little internal logic: names, codes, orders, lists, foreign words, drug stems. Hooks are scaffolding. The goal is fluent retrieval of the real fact, after which the hook fades. A hook that replaces understanding is a liability: it produces confident recall of a label with nothing behind it.

Evidence tags: **Strong** (replicated, meta-analytic or classroom-relevant), **Moderate** (consistent lab evidence, limited transfer or delay data), **Weak** (thin, mixed, or mostly short-delay lab lists), **Craft** (expert practice, not tested as such).

## 0. Rules that override everything else

1. **Meaning before mnemonic.** If the content has a mechanism, teach the mechanism first. Hook only the arbitrary residue.
2. **Every hook must encode true facts.** Check each letter, image and word against the source before giving it. A wrong hook is harder to remove than a wrong fact.
3. **Hooks get tested, not admired.** A hook counts only after the learner retrieves the target from it, unaided, after a delay.
4. **Learner builds or co-builds where possible.** Offer a draft; invite them to replace any part that does not feel like theirs.
5. **No crude, sexual, demeaning or slur-based mnemonics** — even "classic" ones. Offer a clean version that works as well.

## 1. Decide whether a hook is warranted

Ask: *Could the learner reconstruct this from a principle?*

| Material shape | Hook? | What to do instead or as well |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arbitrary names, codes, labels (cranial nerve names, one-letter amino acid codes, SI prefixes) | Yes | Hook + retrieval cards |
| Fixed sequences with no causal order (book order, carpal bones, taxonomic ranks) | Yes | Acrostic, chunking, route |
| Sequences with causal logic (Krebs cycle, action potential, a mechanism) | Partial | Teach the logic; hook only the names; test the logic separately |
| Foreign or technical vocabulary | Yes, briefly | Keyword or etymology hook, then many spaced retrievals and use in context |
| Concepts, mechanisms, derivations, arguments (why competitive inhibition leaves Vmax unchanged, why SN2 inverts) | **No** | Explanation, worked examples, self-explanation, practice problems |
| Procedures and problem-solving (ICE tables, free-body diagrams) | Rarely | A step checklist is fine; a cute acronym for a procedure the learner cannot yet perform is not |

Signals of a crutch: the learner can recite the mnemonic but cannot answer "why"; they must run the whole acrostic to get item 7; they have stacked a mnemonic onto another mnemonic; or the exam asks for application and they only have the list.

## 2. What the evidence says, by hook type

| Technique | What it is | Evidence | What the evidence supports, and its limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Keyword method** | Link a foreign or unfamiliar word to a similar-sounding familiar word, then make an interacting image with the meaning (Spanish *pato* = duck → a duck sitting in a **pot**) | Moderate for immediate recall; Weak for long-term | Atkinson & Raugh (1975, *JEP: Human Learning and Memory* 1:126–133) found large gains for Russian vocabulary. Wang, Thomas & Ouellette (1992, *J Educ Psychol*) found **faster forgetting** with keywords than with rote rehearsal across delays; Wang & Thomas (1995, *J Educ Psychol* 87:468–475) found keyword recall worse than a semantic-context strategy after two days, with the gap much reduced by more review. Dunlosky et al. (2013, *Psychol Sci Public Interest* 14:4–58) rated the keyword mnemonic **low utility** overall. Use it to get a word in; use spaced retrieval to keep it. |
| **Method of loci (memory palace)** | Place vivid images of items along a familiar route; walk the route to recall | Moderate–Strong for serial recall of lists; low-quality trial base | Twomey & Kroneisen (2021, *QJEP* 74:1317–1326): meta-analysis of 13 studies, g = 0.65, with high risk of bias in most trials. Ondřej (2025, *Br J Psychol*, doi:10.1111/bjop.12799): d = 0.88 vs rehearsal for immediate serial recall in adults; evidence graded low to very low quality. Dresler et al. (2017, *Neuron* 93:1227–1235): ~6 weeks of 30-min daily loci training in novices (n = 17 per group) raised recall of 72-word lists from roughly 26–30 to ~62 words, gains persisted at 4 months, and brain connectivity shifted toward memory-athlete patterns; n-back and no-training controls did not show the same gains. Best for ordered lists the learner must produce in sequence. Training cost is real; it does not make understanding. |
| **First-letter acronyms and acrostics** | Initial letters as a cue (OIL RIG; "Some Say Marry Money…") | Weak | Lab results are mixed (e.g., Carlson, Zimmer & Glover 1981, *J Gen Psychol* 104:287–292, titled "First-letter mnemonics: DAM (Don't Aid Memory)"; Morris & Cook 1978, *Br J Educ Psychol* 48:22–28, on when they help). They help mainly with **order and completeness** of items the learner already knows; they do not supply the item itself. A letter that could be three different terms is a weak cue. |
| **Peg system** | Pre-memorized number–image pairs (1 = bun, 2 = shoe…); attach each item to its peg | Weak–Moderate (older list-learning lab studies; not re-verified here) | Useful when position-by-number matters (cranial nerve V, item 7). Requires learning the pegs first. |
| **Chunking** | Group items into meaningful units | Strong as a principle | Working memory holds a few chunks — roughly 4 in Cowan (2001, *Behav Brain Sci* 24:87–114), revising Miller's (1956) "seven plus or minus two". Chunk by meaning when possible (12 prophets → 4 groups of 3). |
| **Elaborative interrogation / elaboration** | Ask "why would this be true?" and connect to known facts | Moderate | Pressley et al. (1988, *J Educ Psychol* 80:268–278) improved learning of "confusing facts"; Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated it moderate utility. This is the meaning-based alternative to a mnemonic, and often the better one. |
| **Self-reference** | Relate the item to yourself | Moderate | Symons & Johnson (1997, *Psychol Bull* 121:371–394) meta-analysis: self-referenced encoding beat semantic encoding on average. Use it in hooks ("the muscle *I* use to…"), not as a personal-data request. |
| **Concreteness, imagery, dual coding** | Pair words with concrete images; use both verbal and visual codes | Moderate | Clark & Paivio (1991, *Educ Psychol Rev* 3:149–210) review the dual coding account. Concrete, interacting images beat abstract words or separate images. Dunlosky et al. rated imagery *for text learning* low utility — imagery helps imageable items, not dense prose. |
| **Bizarreness** | Make images strange | Weak / mixed | McDaniel & Einstein (1986, *JEP:LMC* 12:54–65): bizarre images helped only when mixed with common ones in the same list — a distinctiveness effect, not a general one. Prefer **interacting and distinctive** over merely weird. |
| **Narrative chaining** | Link items into a story | Moderate (lab) | Bower & Clark (1969, *Psychonomic Science* 14:181–182) found large serial-recall gains for lists woven into stories. Good for 6–15 ordered items. |
| **Songs and rhythm** | Set text to a melody | Weak–Moderate; conditional | Wallace (1994, *JEP:LMC* 20:1471–1485) found better verbatim text recall when verses repeated a simple, easily learned melody. Racette & Peretz (2007, *Mem Cognit* 35) found **fewer** words recalled when singing than speaking for unfamiliar songs. Ludke, Ferreira & Overy (2014, *Mem Cognit* 42:41–52) found singing helped adults learn Hungarian phrases. Use only a melody the learner already knows; songs fit verbatim lists, not concepts. |
| **Generation (learner-made hooks)** | The learner produces the cue | Moderate for generation in general; mixed for mnemonics specifically | Bertsch et al. (2007, *Mem Cognit* 35:201–210): across 86 studies, generating beat reading by d ≈ 0.40. For mnemonics, results vary: Ley & Locascio (1977) found subject-generated cues beat experimenter-supplied cues; Lieberman et al. (1968) found no difference. Supplied keywords can win when learners make poor ones. **Default: co-create.** Draft a good hook; let the learner adapt it. |
| **Commercial visual-story mnemonics** (Sketchy, Pixorize, Picmonic) | Illustrated scenes where each object encodes a fact | Weak (limited independent evidence) | No peer-reviewed outcome study of Sketchy or Pixorize was found in a Europe PMC search (2026-10-06). Picmonic has a small single-center randomized pilot (Yang et al. 2014, *Adv Med Educ Pract* 5:125–132) and a non-randomized cohort comparison (Hoyt et al. 2025, *JMIR Med Educ* 11:e55206), both positive. Widely used for pharmacology and microbiology; useful as an image source. Not a replacement for mechanism learning or question practice. |

**Interference** (Moderate–Strong as a principle): similar hooks compete. Two acrostics starting with "S" for two different nerve lists, or two "duck" keywords in one language, will blur. Wang & Thomas attribute part of keyword forgetting to competition from existing associations. Fix: one hook per confusable set, a **contrast card** for the pair, and distinct images.

## 3. Procedure

Run this in order. In chat, one step per turn; the learner does steps 3, 4 and 6.

1. **Gate.** Use §1. If the content is understandable, say so and teach it: "This one has a reason — let's get the reason, and you won't need a trick." Hook only what is left.
2. **Pick the hook by shape.**
   - Unordered set of 3–7 items → acronym, or one interacting scene.
   - Ordered list of 5–15 → acrostic sentence, story chain, or memory palace (palace if they will reuse the list for years).
   - Numbered positions → peg, or a number embedded in the hook ("1973").
   - Word ↔ meaning (foreign, Latin, drug stems) → **etymology first** (real roots are meaning-based and transfer); keyword image only if no usable root.
   - Visual-spatial layout (carpal bones, brachial plexus) → draw it; hook the reading order.
   - Large related sets (bug–drug tables) → one scene per cluster, or a commercial visual set if they already use one.
3. **Build it right.** Concrete, interacting (the items *do something to each other*), distinctive, short, and tied to meaning where possible: the hook should point at *why*, not just *what*. Learner-made or co-made. No more than one layer: a mnemonic for the mnemonic is a stop sign.
4. **Test it.** Hide the hook. Ask for the target from the cue, then the target without the cue. Then a delayed check later in the session, mixed with other items.
5. **Card it.** Put the **target fact** on the card, not the mnemonic. Optionally put the hook on the back as a hint line. Separate cards for meaning ("why does ↑CO₂ shift the curve right?"). See **flashcards** for card rules.
6. **Fade.** When the learner answers fast without running the hook (twice, across a delay), drop the hint line. Keep the hook only for rare items or exam-day recovery.
7. **Retire or repair.** If the hook causes errors (letter maps to the wrong term, image gets confused), replace it, add a contrast card, and say why.

Accuracy check before delivering any hook: list every letter/image and its target; verify each against the course source or a reference; check spelling of the real terms; check the hook does not encode a superseded or contested fact without saying so.

## 4. Worked examples

Each is accurate as written; each names what the hook does *not* teach.

**1. Cranial nerve names, in order (anatomy).** "**O**h **O**h **O**h **T**o **T**ouch **A**nd **F**eel **V**ery **G**ood **V**elvet, **A**h **H**eaven" → olfactory, optic, oculomotor, trochlear, trigeminal, abducens, facial, vestibulocochlear, glossopharyngeal, vagus, accessory, hypoglossal. Weakness: three O's and two T's — pair it with a peg or position check ("V is trigeminal, VI is abducens").

**2. Cranial nerve modality.** "**S**ome **S**ay **M**arry **M**oney **B**ut **M**y **B**rother **S**ays **B**ig **B**rains **M**atter **M**ore" → I S, II S, III M, IV M, V B, VI M, VII B, VIII S, IX B, X B, XI M, XII M (sensory/motor/both). Add a number hook: parasympathetic fibers ride on **10, 9, 7, 3** — "**1973**." Not taught: what each nerve does; teach that from function and lesions.

**3. Right shift of the O₂–hemoglobin curve (physiology).** "**CADET, face Right**": **C**O₂ ↑, **A**cid (↑H⁺, ↓pH), **D**PG (2,3-BPG) ↑, **E**xercise, **T**emperature ↑ → lower affinity, more O₂ unloading. Meaning layer (teach first): every item is a signature of hard-working tissue, so the shift delivers O₂ where it is needed. "Exercise" is a context, not a molecule; it works through the other four. If the learner can derive the list from "what does active muscle look like?", drop the acronym.

**4. Amino acid one-letter codes (biochemistry).** Eleven match their first letter (A, C, G, H, I, L, M, P, S, T, V). Hook the other nine: **R** = "aRginine" (pirate "aRRR"), **K** = lysine (K sits next to L), **D** = "asparDic" acid, **E** = "glutEmate", **N** = "asparagiNe", **Q** = "Q-tamine" (sounds like "cute-amine") for glutamine, **F** = "Fenylalanine", **Y** = "tYrosine", **W** = tryptophan (the **double** ring of indole → "double-u"). Then card side-chain *properties* separately by structure, not by hook.

**5. Essential amino acids — a hook that needs a footnote.** The common "**PVT TIM HALL**" (Phe, Val, Thr, Trp, Ile, Met, His, Arg, Leu, Lys) lists ten. The standard adult list is nine; arginine is **conditionally** essential. Teach the nine, keep the hook only if the learner annotates "R = conditional." This is the pattern for every inherited mnemonic: audit before reuse.

**6. Krebs cycle intermediates (biochemistry).** "**C**itrate **I**s **K**rebs' **S**tarting **S**ubstrate **F**or **M**aking **O**xaloacetate" → citrate, isocitrate, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl-CoA, succinate, fumarate, malate, oxaloacetate. Hook = names and order only. The logic is taught and tested separately: CO₂ leaves at the two decarboxylations (isocitrate → α-KG, α-KG → succinyl-CoA), NADH at those two plus malate → OAA, FADH₂ at succinate → fumarate, GTP/ATP at succinyl-CoA → succinate. A memory-palace version (eight stations in the learner's kitchen) suits someone who will need it for years.

**7. Carpal bones (anatomy) — clean replacement.** "**S**o **L**ong **T**o **P**inky, **H**ere **C**omes **T**he **T**humb" → proximal row lateral to medial: scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum, pisiform; distal row medial to lateral: hamate, capitate, trapezoid, trapezium (anatomical position). Trapezi**um** sits by the th**um**b. Draw the hand and trace the U-shaped path; the drawing does most of the work.

**8. Formal charge and hydrogen bonding (general/organic chemistry).** Formal charge = valence electrons − **dots** − **sticks** (lone-pair electrons, and one per bond). O in H₃O⁺: 6 − 2 − 3 = +1. N in NH₄⁺: 5 − 0 − 4 = +1. Hydrogen-bond donors: H on **FON** (F, O or N). Both hooks compress a rule the learner should also be able to explain (electrons "owned" vs. shared; electronegativity and small size). Lewis-structure *strategy* (count electrons, connect, complete octets, minimize formal charge) is a procedure to practice, not to acronym.

**9. Oxidation and reduction (chemistry, biochemistry).** "**OIL RIG**": Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). Bridge to organic and biochemical usage: carbon is oxidized when it gains bonds to O (or loses bonds to H); NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH. Test with "Is ethanol → acetaldehyde an oxidation? Which coenzyme is reduced?"

**10. Metric prefixes (physics, chemistry).** "**K**ing **H**enry **D**ied **B**y **D**rinking **C**hocolate **M**ilk" → kilo (10³), hecto (10²), deka (10¹), base unit, deci (10⁻¹), centi (10⁻²), milli (10⁻³). Each step is ×10, so conversion = counting steps. Extend with micro (10⁻⁶) and nano (10⁻⁹) by chunks of three. Physics content itself (F = ma, energy conservation) gets **no** mnemonic: derive and apply.

**11. The Twelve (minor) prophets, in Protestant English and Masoretic order (biblical studies).** Chunk into four triads and say them rhythmically: **Hosea–Joel–Amos / Obadiah–Jonah–Micah / Nahum–Habakkuk–Zephaniah / Haggai–Zechariah–Malachi**. Meaning layer: the last triad is post-exilic. Tradition note: Septuagint-based (Eastern Orthodox) Bibles order the first six Hosea, Amos, Micah, Joel, Obadiah, Jonah — ask which canon the course uses before drilling.

**12. Latin and theological terms — etymology beats keywords.** *Lex orandi, lex credendi* ("the law of praying [is] the law of believing"): *orandi* → orate, oration; *credendi* → creed, credible. *Creatio ex nihilo*: *nihil* → nihilism, annihilate ("from nothing"). *Imago Dei*: image of God. Real roots carry meaning into new words; a sound-alike keyword ("ex Neil-ho") does not. Use keyword images only for words with no usable root, and then space the retrievals.

## 5. Anti-patterns

- **Crude, sexual, demeaning, or slur-based mnemonics**, including widely circulated med-school ones. They alienate, embarrass learners in group study, and are unnecessary — a clean hook of the same shape works.
- **Hooks that encode wrong or outdated facts** (PVT TIM HALL without the arginine note; a taxonomy acrostic presented as if every kingdom scheme agreed). Audit inherited mnemonics.
- **Too many layers**: a mnemonic to remember which mnemonic, nested acronyms, or one acrostic per lecture slide. Each layer is another thing to forget.
- **Mnemonics for understanding-based content**: acronyms for mechanisms, derivations, philosophical arguments, or clinical reasoning. If a "why" question breaks it, it was the wrong tool.
- **Mnemonic before meaning**: memorizing CADET before knowing what a right shift *is*.
- **Hook-only cards**: a card that asks for the mnemonic instead of the fact.
- **Ambiguous letters**: "M" that could be three terms in the same chapter.
- **Confusable hook sets** without a contrast card.
- **Claiming permanence**: hooks speed initial learning; without spaced retrieval, they can be forgotten faster than rote-learned material (Wang et al. 1992).
- **Never retiring**: running a 12-word acrostic in an exam for an item that should be instant.

## 6. Closing a hook session

1. Learner recalls each target **without** its hook, mixed order.
2. Status per item (see **tutor** §8): hinted recall is not independent recall.
3. Cards made for targets (hook as optional hint line), plus "why" cards.
4. Next review proposed: tomorrow-ish, a few days, about a week. A suggestion is not a reminder; schedule only with a real scheduling tool, and verify it was created.
