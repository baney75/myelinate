# Teaching and tutoring methods: what the best evidence supports

Source review: 2026-10-06; tutoring-dialogue and sycophancy rows added 2026-10-10. For the mechanisms underneath these methods (prediction error, generation, consolidation, metacognitive illusions, animal myelination evidence and its limits), see `how-the-mind-learns.md`. Strength tags: **Strong** (meta-analyses or replicated controlled studies), **Moderate** (one good RCT or consistent lab evidence), **Weak** (single study, preprint, observational), **Craft** (expert practice without outcome data). Components are supported; Myelinate as a whole is not an evaluated intervention.

## Designing a course or unit

| Method | What to do | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backward design (Wiggins & McTighe, *Understanding by Design*, 1998/2005) | Start from the success test, decide what evidence would prove it, then choose activities | Craft (widely adopted design framework) |
| Rosenshine, "Principles of Instruction," *American Educator* 2012 | Daily review; small steps; many questions; model; guided practice; check understanding; ~80% success in practice; scaffolds; independent practice; weekly/monthly review | Moderate–Strong (synthesis of cognitive science, master-teacher, and scaffolding research) |
| Explicit instruction for novices (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006; Alfieri et al. 2011) | Teach new material directly with worked examples; unassisted discovery loses (d ≈ −0.38); enhanced/guided discovery beats other instruction (d ≈ +0.30) | Strong |
| Invent-then-tell for prepared learners (Schwartz & Bransford 1998; Schwartz et al. 2011; Sinha & Kapur 2021) | Contrasting cases or a challenging problem before the formal idea — best for conceptual understanding and transfer | Moderate |
| Worked examples and fading (Sweller; Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill 2003); expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al. 2003) | Full worked example → completion problems → independent; drop guidance as skill grows | Strong |
| Cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman 1989) | Make expert thinking visible: model, coach, scaffold, articulate, reflect, explore | Craft–Moderate |

## Checking and adjusting

| Method | What to do | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formative assessment (Black & Wiliam 1998, "Inside the Black Box"; Wiliam 2011, *Embedded Formative Assessment*) | Collect evidence of thinking during teaching and change what you do next | Moderate–Strong (effect sizes debated) |
| Hinge questions (Wiliam) | At a decision point, one quick item whose wrong options each reveal a specific misconception | Craft built on the above |
| Peer Instruction / ConcepTests (Mazur 1997; Crouch & Mazur 2001) | Commit answer → discuss → re-answer; in 1:1, the learner argues against a plausible wrong view | Moderate–Strong in physics |
| "Does that make sense?" is unreliable (Person, Graesser et al. 1994) | Check by production: apply, predict, explain, find the error | Moderate |
| Feedback (Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020) | High-information feedback d ≈ 0.99; corrective (right/wrong + answer) 0.46; reinforcement/punishment (praise or reward without task information) 0.24 | Strong |

## Making it stick

| Method | What to do | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retrieval practice (Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Dunlosky et al. 2013) | Recall without notes; close with the learner's recall, not a summary | Strong |
| Spacing (Cepeda et al. 2006, 2008) | Optimal gap ≈ 20–40% of a 1-week horizon, ≈ 5–10% of a 1-year horizon | Strong |
| Successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky 2011; Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013) | Initial criterion of 3 correct recalls, then relearn to 1 correct in each of 3+ spaced sessions | Strong |
| Interleaving (Brunmair & Richter 2019) | Mix confusable categories once basics exist (g ≈ 0.42; larger for similar categories) | Moderate–Strong |
| Self-explanation (Bisra et al. 2018) | Open "why/how" prompts, g ≈ 0.55 | Strong |
| Learning by teaching (Fiorella & Mayer 2013) | Explain to another (teach-back) | Moderate |
| Pretesting / prequestions (St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* meta-analysis) | Ask before teaching; even wrong guesses help later learning of the questioned material | Moderate |
| Generation effect (Bertsch et al. 2007, 86 studies) and errorful generation with feedback (Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009; Metcalfe 2017) | Get an attempt before telling; always follow an error with feedback | Strong (lab) |
| Hypercorrection (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001) | Ask confidence before the verdict; confident errors corrected with explanation stick | Moderate |
| Productive failure (Sinha & Kapur 2021, 53 studies) | For prepared learners, a well-designed problem before instruction helps conceptual knowledge and transfer; not for young children or procedures | Moderate–Strong |
| ICAP (Chi & Wylie 2014) | Interactive > constructive > active > passive engagement; reading an explanation is at best active | Moderate |
| Metacognitive illusions (Kornell & Bjork 2008; Deslauriers et al. 2019; Rozenblit & Keil 2002; Dunlosky & Rawson 2012) | Effortful learning feels worse; "I get it" overstates understanding; overconfidence ends study early. Test, don't ask | Strong |
| Wise feedback (Yeager et al. 2014) | High standards plus explicit assurance the learner can meet them | Moderate |

## One-on-one tutoring

| Finding | Implication |
| --- | --- |
| Tutoring averages ≈ 0.37 SD across 96 RCTs in the 2020 working paper and ≈ 0.29 SD across 89 PreK–12 RCTs in the 2024 update (Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan; use the 2024 figure); Bloom's "2 sigma" relied on narrow tests and bundled mastery learning (critique: von Hippel 2024, *Education Next*; VanLehn 2011) | Expect real but not magical gains; structure and dosage matter |
| Step-based tutoring ≈ human tutoring (VanLehn 2011: 0.76 vs 0.79) | Feedback at each step captures most of the benefit |
| Prompting-only tutors did as well as explaining tutors (Chi et al. 2001) | The learner's construction drives learning |
| Expert tutors are nurturant, indirect with errors, Socratic, progressive (Lepper's INSPIRE) | Questions over lectures; indirect error cues first |
| Answer-giving AI: practice +48%, exam −17% after removal; guardrailed tutor avoided most harm (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025, ~1,000 high-school math students) | Never lead with the full solution; hints, not answers |
| Structured AI tutor with pre-written solutions, brief turns, one step at a time produced larger immediate post-test gains in less time than in-class active learning (Kestin et al., Sci Rep 2025; one Harvard physics course, N = 194, crossover; the effect size depends on the analysis; a January 2026 re-analysis by Clay Ford, clayford.net/posts/ai_tutoring_nature, questions the "more than double" framing) | Verified solutions in context; brevity; stepwise reveal |
| Answer leakage is common: ChatGPT revealed the solution in 66% of MathDial tutoring turns (Macina et al. 2023, arXiv:2305.14536); GPT-4 was non-revealing in about 53% of MRBench responses against about 91% for expert humans (Maurya et al., NAACL 2025, arXiv:2412.09416) | Leak-check every draft; key in private |
| Good tutor responses identify the mistake, locate it, avoid revealing the answer, give correct guidance, make the next step actionable, stay coherent, and keep an encouraging tone (seven of MRBench's eight dimensions; the eighth, human-likeness, is omitted; BEA 2025 shared task, arXiv:2507.10579) | Use these as acceptance criteria for tutoring replies |
| Expert remediation decisions (identify the error type, choose a strategy and intention) made GPT-4 responses much more preferred (Bridge, Wang et al., NAACL 2024) | Diagnose the kind of miss before choosing the help |
| LLM help that *substitutes* (writes the solution) raised coverage but lowered understanding; help that *complements* (explains after the learner's work) helped (Lehmann, Cornelius & Sting, arXiv:2409.09047) | Explain after an attempt, never instead of one |
| ChatGPT-assisted writers improved their essays most but gained no more knowledge or transfer than controls ("metacognitive laziness", Fan et al., BJET 2025, N = 117) | A better product is not more learning |
| Models flipped answers on ~46% of "are you sure?" challenges (Laban et al. 2023, arXiv:2311.08596); sycophancy rates ~58% across math and medical items (Fanous et al., SycEval, AIES 2025); models preserve users' "face" far more than humans do (Cheng et al. 2025, ELEPHANT) | Key-first verdicts; re-solve, then hold |
| Novices tutored by a highly sycophantic chatbot corrected fewer misconceptions and mostly didn't notice (Bo et al., CHI 2026, N = 24) | Agreement can hurt without anyone noticing |
| Mentioning a wrong answer in the prompt lowers model accuracy on checking it (Arvin 2025, arXiv:2506.10297) | Solve before reading the learner's claimed answer |
| Contingent tutoring (more help after failure, less after success; Wood & Middleton 1975) is a classic heuristic, but a 2025 preregistered replication with toddlers did not reproduce its effect (Smit et al., J Educ Psychol) | Use the ladder; watch what actually helps |
| LLMs are sycophantic — they tend to agree with users and flip correct answers under pushback (Sharma et al. 2023, arXiv:2310.13548) | Hold correct positions; change only on evidence |
| Long spoken explanations do worse than the same text (transient-information effect; Leahy & Sweller 2011); spoken vs typed input made little learning difference in computer tutoring (Litman et al. 2006) | In voice: 1–3 sentences per turn, say-back checks |

## Motivation without hype

- Praise process and strategy, not ability (Mueller & Dweck 1998). Growth-mindset *interventions* have small and contested effects (Sisk et al. 2018; Yeager et al. 2019; Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023): use process language, skip the lectures.
- Autonomy: offer real choices of next task (Lepper's INSPIRE; self-determination theory).
- Attribute difficulty to the material and strategy, which can change.

## Myths to avoid

- Learning styles (matching instruction to a "visual/auditory/kinesthetic" type): no adequate evidence (Pashler et al. 2008). Respect preferences; match the representation to the content.
- "Rereading and highlighting" as study: low utility (Dunlosky et al. 2013).
- "Fluent with help = learned": performance during assisted practice overstates learning (Bastani et al. 2025; Bjork's desirable difficulties).
